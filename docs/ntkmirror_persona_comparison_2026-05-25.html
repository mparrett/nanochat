Parity, not victory

NTK-Mirror lifts Qwen by +27pp on persona-retention but matches L1 LoRA after seed bracketing.

nanochat · experiment/hope-nested-learning · 2026-05-25

Yesterday's NTK-Mirror smoke succeeded on disjoint-task composition. Today we ran the cross-adapter comparison on persona-retention against the L1 LoRA v2 reference (19/30 all_three). A single-seed reading came in at 20/30 — the "beats LoRA" headline. Seed bracketing collapsed it: mean 18/30 across n=3, range 17–20. The robust claim is the +27pp lift over Qwen base; the L1 LoRA comparison lands at parity, not victory. Per the ticket's pass criteria, Path B graft is shelved.

What we ran

Path A from the cross-adapter ticket: NTK-Mirror in its native habitat (Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct) vs the L1 LoRA v2 reference on d6_baseline_modern_sft. The shared substrate is the persona-retention dataset (300 train / 30 eval, curated 2026-05-07 via the operator's Claude subscription auth) and the substring scoring rubric from dev/eval_persona_retention.py.

Dataset adaptation (bench/adapt_persona_to_qwen.py) renders each 6-turn conversation via Qwen's chat template: 

prompt    = tok.apply_chat_template(msgs[:5], add_generation_prompt=True)
            # ends at "<|im_start|>assistant\n"
completion= full[len(prompt):]
            # T6 assistant content + "<|im_end|>\n"

Token budgets land comfortably under --max-length 256 (train max 236, eval max 181). The eval JSONL carries the original _persona dict through so the same substring scoring used by eval_persona_retention.py applies bit-for-bit.

Fit setup

ntkmirror fit --model Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct --device mps \
  --train train.jsonl --out persona_controller.pt \
  --gates 4000 --steps 240 --lr 5e-3 \
  --max-length 256 --score-batches 8 --batch-size 8

Dial bundle from the ticket: --max-length 256, --score-batches 8, --gates 4000 (vs default 5000). Memory was tight (0.73 GB free at launch) but the dialed-down bundle plus shorter persona prompts (~150 tokens vs ~300 for yesterday's GSM8K) brought step time down to ~2.8 s — six× faster than yesterday's worst case.

Seed bracketing — three fits

NTK-Mirror's fit is fully deterministic given a fixed batch ordering: gate selection is argmax|∂L/∂s| over the first score_batches chunks; raw inits to zero; the training loop cycles range(steps) through train_batches with no shuffle. So train order is the only knob that injects variance.

bench/shuffle_train.py produces deterministic reorderings. Three fits: s0 (default order from adapt script), s1 (random.Random(1).shuffle), s2 (random.Random(2)).

Per-row consistency across NTK seeds

  • 16/30 rows pass on all three seeds — the robust-wins floor.
  • 21/30 rows pass on at least one seed — the upper envelope.
  • 9/30 rows fail across all seeds (3, 10, 11, 16, 19, 20, 21, 22, 27) — persistent failures.

Different seeds flip different rows. s0 catches rows 8 and 24 that s1/s2 miss; s2 catches row 14 that s0/s1 miss. The variance isn't noise on a fixed result — it's genuine instability in which examples the controller transfers to.

s1's fit barely converged — train loss only fell 15%. Yet its eval score is identical to s2's (which fell 38%). Eval score is bounded by what gates got selected; once a suboptimal sparse mask is fixed, no amount of further AdamW on raw recovers.

Mechanism characteristic worth flagging upstream. Gate selection is a one-shot pass over the first score_batches chunks. Which 64 examples land in that scoring window directly determines which 4000 channels get chosen, and subsequent training can only adjust their magnitudes, not switch selection. A bad first-64 → a ceiling on what training can recover. Stratified or learned sampling for the scoring pass would likely reduce this variance substantially.

Failure-mode shift

The qualitatively most interesting finding isn't the score — it's what the controller does to which failures.

The dominant Qwen base failure mode is the instruct-tune identity attractor: "I am Qwen, an AI language model created by Alibaba Cloud." Fires in 7/30 base rows. NTK-Mirror suppresses it almost completely — only row 16 still deflects across all seeds. That's a sparse channel intervention reshaping a strong attractor without retraining weights, which is the mechanism's headline claim.

The remaining 9 persistent failures decompose as:

  • Topic-continuation dodge (rows 11, 14, 21, 22) — model continues about the conversation topic instead of answering the recall question.
  • Partial recall — name only (rows 10, 20).
  • Persistent Qwen deflection (row 16) — the one attractor channel the gate selection didn't pick up.
  • Truncated greedy decode (row 27) — outputs "Isabella Wong" then EOS.
  • Substring brittleness (row 19) — model says "owner of a craft brewery in Melbourne" but the persona role string is "craft brewery owner". Content correct, scoring strict.

Interpretation

What survives bracketing

Two claims are robust across all three seeds:

  1. NTK-Mirror does something real at this scale. Mean +27pp over Qwen base (17/30, 17/30, 20/30 vs 10/30). 16 rows pass on every seed.
  2. NTK-Mirror is roughly competitive with LoRA at this scale. Mean 18.0/30 vs LoRA's 19/30. Not a clean win, not a clean loss.

What doesn't

The 20/30 single-seed reading. Without bracketing, this writeup would have led with "NTK-Mirror beats LoRA." The ticket anticipated exactly this pattern when it specified "13–19 → noisy → need n=2–3 seed bracketing." It was right to.

Cross-base attribution caveat

L1 LoRA v2's 19/30 was on d6_baseline_modern_sft (~85M params, nanochat tokenizer, custom SFT recipe). NTK-Mirror's 18/30 was on Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (~500M params, Qwen tokenizer, Qwen instruct-tune). The two numbers share a dataset and a scoring rubric and not much else.

What we can claim: both adapters substantially lift their respective bases on this task. The adapter footprint differs by ~400× — NTK-Mirror's 4000 sparse floats (~16 KB) vs LoRA's rank-16 Q+K+V+O (~6 M params, ~24 MB on disk) — without a corresponding gap in performance.

What we can't claim: that either mechanism is intrinsically superior to the other. That requires Path B (same base, same data, same recipe) — which we're now shelving.

Decision

Ticket pass criteria: beats 19/30 → activation-space superior; 13–19 → inconclusive, need bracketing; ≤13 → LoRA wins cleanly.

We did the bracketing. Mean lands at 18 — one row below the threshold, inside the "inconclusive" band. Path B (graft into nanochat, ~1 d port) is not justified by the cross-adapter signal. It's also not ruled out — parity at 400× smaller adapter is a real architectural difference. But the cost/signal ratio doesn't clear the bar for committing engineering now.

Shelving Path B. Reopen if a downstream need (composability, persistent-memory store, sparse-dict adapters, bench-v0 third arm) makes the graft asset itself worth the engineering — at that point the parity result becomes a feature rather than a forcing function.
